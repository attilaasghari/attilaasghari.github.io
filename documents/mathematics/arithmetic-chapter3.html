Chapter 3: Fractions and Mixed Numbers

“A fraction is not a flaw—it’s a feature of reality. Most things in life aren’t whole.”

— Professor’s Notebook

You’ve split a pizza, measured ingredients, or checked how much battery remains on your phone. In each case, you’ve used fractions—numbers that represent parts of a whole. This chapter teaches you to read, simplify, compare, and compute with fractions confidently and accurately.

3.1 Understanding Fractions and Their Types

A fraction has two parts:

  • Numerator (top): how many parts you have
  • Denominator (bottom): how many equal parts the whole is divided into
\[ \frac{\text{Numerator}}{\text{Denominator}} = \frac{3}{4} \quad \text{means “3 out of 4 equal parts”} \]

Types of Fractions

  1. Proper Fraction: Numerator < Denominator
    → \(\frac{2}{5},\ \frac{7}{10}\)
    → Always less than 1.
  2. Improper Fraction: Numerator ≥ Denominator
    → \(\frac{9}{4},\ \frac{15}{15}\)
    → Equal to or greater than 1.
  3. Mixed Number: A whole number + a proper fraction
    → \(2\frac{3}{4},\ 5\frac{1}{2}\)
    → Common in cooking, construction, and time (e.g., “2½ hours”).

Visual Aid:
Imagine a chocolate bar divided into 8 equal squares.
– \(\frac{5}{8}\): You ate 5 squares.
– \(\frac{8}{8} = 1\): You ate the whole bar.
– \(\frac{11}{8} = 1\frac{3}{8}\): You ate one full bar and 3 squares of a second.

3.2 Equivalent Fractions and Simplification

Equivalent fractions look different but represent the same value.

Example:

\[ \frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{50}{100} \]

Why? Because you multiplied (or divided) both numerator and denominator by the same number—you’re just cutting the whole into finer or coarser pieces.

Simplifying (Reducing) Fractions

To simplify, divide numerator and denominator by their greatest common divisor (GCD).

Example: Simplify \(\frac{18}{24}\)

  • GCD of 18 and 24 is 6
  • \(\frac{18 \div 6}{24 \div 6} = \frac{3}{4}\)

✅ Simplest form: When numerator and denominator share no common factor other than 1.

Practical Tip:
Always simplify your final answer—it’s like cleaning your workspace.
In recipes, \(\frac{4}{8}\) cup is correct, but \(\frac{1}{2}\) cup is clearer.

3.3 Operations with Fractions

Addition & Subtraction

Rule: Denominators must be the same.

Step 1: Find a common denominator (usually the least common denominator, or LCD).
Step 2: Convert each fraction.
Step 3: Add/subtract numerators. Keep denominator.

Example:

\[ \frac{2}{3} + \frac{5}{6} \]
  • LCD of 3 and 6 is 6
  • \(\frac{2}{3} = \frac{4}{6}\)
  • \(\frac{4}{6} + \frac{5}{6} = \frac{9}{6} = \frac{3}{2} = 1\frac{1}{2}\)

Real-world use:
You ran \(\frac{3}{4}\) mile in the morning and \(\frac{2}{3}\) mile in the evening. Total distance?
→ \(\frac{3}{4} + \frac{2}{3} = \frac{9}{12} + \frac{8}{12} = \frac{17}{12} = 1\frac{5}{12}\) miles.

Multiplication

No common denominator needed!
Multiply numerators, then denominators.

\[ \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \cdot c}{b \cdot d} \]

Example:

\[ \frac{2}{5} \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{6}{20} = \frac{3}{10} \]

Shortcut: Cancel before multiplying (simplify across).
\(\frac{2}{5} \times \frac{15}{8} = \frac{\cancel{2}^1}{\cancel{5}^1} \times \frac{\cancel{15}^3}{\cancel{8}^4} = \frac{1 \cdot 3}{1 \cdot 4} = \frac{3}{4}\)

Division

“Keep, Change, Flip”

  • Keep the first fraction
  • Change ÷ to ×
  • Flip (take reciprocal of) the second fraction
\[ \frac{3}{4} \div \frac{2}{5} = \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{5}{2} = \frac{15}{8} = 1\frac{7}{8} \]

Why it works:
Dividing by \(\frac{2}{5}\) means “how many \(\frac{2}{5}\)s fit into \(\frac{3}{4}\)?”
Multiplying by \(\frac{5}{2}\) answers that question.

3.4 Converting Between Improper Fractions and Mixed Numbers

Mixed Number → Improper Fraction

Steps:

  1. Multiply whole number by denominator
  2. Add numerator
  3. Put result over original denominator

Example:

\[ 2\frac{3}{4} = \frac{(2 \times 4) + 3}{4} = \frac{8 + 3}{4} = \frac{11}{4} \]

Improper Fraction → Mixed Number

Steps:

  1. Divide numerator by denominator
  2. Quotient = whole number
  3. Remainder = new numerator
  4. Denominator stays the same

Example:

\[ \frac{17}{5} \rightarrow 17 \div 5 = 3 \text{ R }2 \Rightarrow 3\frac{2}{5} \]

When to use which?
– Mixed numbers: Everyday communication (“I need 1½ cups of flour”)
– Improper fractions: Math calculations (easier to add, multiply, etc.)

Chapter Summary

  • Fractions represent parts of a whole—proper, improper, or mixed.
  • Equivalent fractions are equal in value; simplify by dividing by the GCD.
  • Operations:
    • Add/subtract → common denominator
    • Multiply → straight across (simplify first!)
    • Divide → “keep, change, flip”
  • Conversions: Switch between mixed and improper forms as needed for clarity or computation.

  5. Convert \(3\frac{5}{7}\) to an improper fraction.
  6. Convert \(\frac{29}{6}\) to a mixed number.
