1. Arithmetic

Chapter 1: The Language of Numbers

“Mathematics is not about numbers, equations, or algorithms: it is about understanding.”

— William Paul Thurston

Before we solve equations, draw shapes, or model the motion of planets, we must first speak the language in which all of mathematics is written: numbers. This chapter introduces the basic vocabulary—what numbers are, how we write them, and how we use them to make sense of the world.

1.1 Introduction to Numbers: Natural, Whole, and Integers

Natural Numbers

These are the counting numbers:

\[ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ \dots \]

You use them when you count apples, steps, or days. They start at 1 and go on forever. (Note: Some older texts include 0 in natural numbers, but in modern usage—especially in computer science and number theory—we usually start at 1.)

Whole Numbers

Add zero to the natural numbers, and you get the whole numbers:

\[ 0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ \dots \]

Zero represents “nothing,” but it’s a powerful idea—it lets us talk about empty sets, balances of zero dollars, or starting points on a number line.

Integers

Now imagine debt, temperature below zero, or floors below ground level in a building. To describe these, we need negative numbers. Combine positives, zero, and negatives, and you get the integers:

\[ \dots,\ -3,\ -2,\ -1,\ 0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ \dots \]

Integers have no fractional or decimal parts. They are complete “steps” along the number line.

Practical Insight:
When your bank account shows –$25, that’s an integer. When a thermometer reads –10°C, that’s also an integer. The number line isn’t just abstract—it’s your daily reality.

1.2 Place Value and Number Forms

Numbers are written using a base-10 system. This means each digit’s value depends on its place.

Take the number 3,427:

Thousands Hundreds Tens Ones
3 4 2 7
  • The 3 means 3 × 1,000 = 3,000
  • The 4 means 4 × 100 = 400
  • The 2 means 2 × 10 = 20
  • The 7 means 7 × 1 = 7

So,

\[ 3,427 = 3{,}000 + 400 + 20 + 7 \]

This is called expanded form. It reveals the hidden structure of numbers.

We can also write numbers in:

  • Standard form: 3,427
  • Word form: three thousand four hundred twenty-seven
  • Expanded form: 3,000 + 400 + 20 + 7

Why it matters:
Place value is essential for addition, subtraction, and understanding large data (like national budgets or scientific measurements). Misreading a digit’s place can turn a million into a thousand!

1.3 Rounding and Estimation

Exact numbers are great—but sometimes, close enough is smarter, faster, and more useful.

Rounding means simplifying a number to a nearby “friendly” value.

Rule of thumb:

  • Look at the digit to the right of your target place.
  • If it’s 5 or more, round up.
  • If it’s 4 or less, keep the digit the same (round down).

Example: Round 4,872 to the nearest hundred.

  • The hundreds digit is 8.
  • The digit to its right is 7 (≥5), so round up:
\[ 4,872 \approx 4,900 \]

Estimation uses rounding to make quick calculations.

Real-world use:
You’re at the grocery store with $50. Your items cost $12.99, $8.49, and $27.95.
Round: $13 + $8 + $28 = $49 → You’re under budget!
No calculator needed.

Caution: Estimation gives you a ballpark, not a blueprint. Use it for decisions, not precision engineering.

Chapter Summary

  • Natural numbers: for counting (1, 2, 3, …)
  • Whole numbers: natural + zero
  • Integers: whole numbers + negatives
  • Place value tells you what each digit really means
  • Rounding and estimation help you think quickly and wisely
