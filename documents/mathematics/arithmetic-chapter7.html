Chapter 7: Geometry in Arithmetic

“Geometry is the art of reasoning well from badly drawn figures.”

— Henri Poincaré
(But with good arithmetic, your reasoning will be excellent.)

You don’t need advanced math to measure a room, compare pizza sizes, or estimate how much paint to buy. This chapter shows how arithmetic powers geometry—turning shapes into numbers you can add, multiply, and compare. We’ll focus on perimeter, area, and volume of common figures, using the operations, fractions, decimals, and ratios you already know.

7.1 Basic Geometric Shapes and Terms

Before we calculate, let’s speak the language of space.

  • Point: A location (no size).
  • Line: Straight, infinitely long, no thickness.
  • Line segment: Part of a line with two endpoints.
  • Angle: Formed by two rays sharing a vertex. Measured in degrees (°).
    • Right angle = 90°
    • Straight angle = 180°
    • Acute < 90°, Obtuse > 90°
  • Perimeter: Total distance around a 2D shape.
  • Area: Amount of surface inside a 2D shape (measured in square units: cm², ft², etc.).
  • Volume: Amount of space inside a 3D object (measured in cubic units: m³, in³, etc.).

Key Insight:
Perimeter → adding lengths
Area → multiplying lengths
Volume → multiplying three lengths

7.2 Using Arithmetic in Geometry

Now, let’s compute using formulas—all built from basic arithmetic.

Rectangle

  • Perimeter: \( P = 2 \times (\text{length} + \text{width}) \)
  • Area: \( A = \text{length} \times \text{width} \)

Example: A garden is 12.5 ft by 8 ft.

  • Perimeter: \(2 \times (12.5 + 8) = 2 \times 20.5 = 41\) ft
  • Area: \(12.5 \times 8 = 100\) ft²

Triangle

  • Area: \( A = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} \)

Why \(\frac{1}{2}\)? A triangle is half of a rectangle with the same base and height.

Example: Base = 10 cm, height = 6 cm

\[ A = \frac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 6 = 30\ \text{cm}^2 \]

Circle

  • Radius (\(r\)): distance from center to edge
  • Diameter (\(d\)): \(d = 2r\)
  • Circumference (perimeter): \( C = \pi d = 2\pi r \)
  • Area: \( A = \pi r^2 \)

Use \( \pi \approx 3.14 \) or \( \frac{22}{7} \) for estimation.

Example: Pizza with radius 8 inches

\[ A = \pi \times 8^2 = 64\pi \approx 64 \times 3.14 = 200.96\ \text{in}^2 \]

Practical Insight:
A 16-inch pizza (diameter) has four times the area of an 8-inch pizza—not double!
Because area depends on \(r^2\): \((8)^2 = 64\) vs. \((4)^2 = 16\) → 64/16 = 4.

Rectangular Prism (Box)

  • Volume: \( V = \text{length} \times \text{width} \times \text{height} \)

Example: Shipping box: 12 in × 8 in × 5 in

\[ V = 12 \times 8 \times 5 = 480\ \text{in}^3 \]

Real-world use:
Moving companies charge by volume—knowing this helps you estimate costs.

7.3 Applying Ratios and Percents in Geometric Contexts

Geometry isn’t just about absolute size—it’s about relationships.

1. Scale Drawings and Maps

A blueprint uses a scale ratio, like 1 in : 10 ft.

Problem: On a map, 1 cm = 5 km. Two cities are 4.2 cm apart.

  • Actual distance = \(4.2 \times 5 = 21\) km

→ This is a proportional relationship—same as in Chapter 5.

2. Percent Change in Area or Volume

If you double the side of a square, what happens to its area?

  • Original side = \(s\) → area = \(s^2\)
  • New side = \(2s\) → area = \((2s)^2 = 4s^2\)

✅ Area becomes 4 times larger → a 300% increase.

Similarly:

  • Double all dimensions of a box → volume increases by \(2^3 = 8\) times (700% increase).

Why it matters:
A “20% larger” container might mean 20% taller—but if width and depth stay the same, volume only increases by 20%. Always check what is being scaled!

3. Percent of a Shape

A circular garden has a 6 ft radius. A 90° sprinkler covers one-quarter of it. What percent is covered?

  • Full circle = 360°
  • \( \frac{90}{360} = \frac{1}{4} = 25\% \)

Or: If a triangle takes up 18 in² of a 60 in² poster,

\[ \frac{18}{60} = 0.3 = 30\% \]

Chapter Summary

  • Perimeter = sum of sides; Area = surface (use formulas); Volume = 3D space
  • Key formulas rely on multiplication, fractions, and π
  • Circles grow with the square of the radius—bigger than they seem!
  • Ratios govern scale drawings; percents describe parts of shapes or changes in size

Practice Problems

  1. Find the perimeter and area of a rectangle 7.2 cm by 4.5 cm.
  2. A triangle has base 15 m and height 8 m. What’s its area?
  3. A circle has diameter 14 cm. Find its circumference and area (use \( \pi = \frac{22}{7} \)).
  4. A cube has side length 6 in. What’s its volume?
  5. On a scale drawing, 1 inch = 3 feet. A room is 4.5 inches long on the plan. How long is it in reality?
  6. If you triple the radius of a circle, by what percent does the area increase?
  7. A 12-inch pizza costs \$10. An 18-inch pizza costs \$18. Which is the better value per square inch?
