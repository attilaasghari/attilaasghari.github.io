Chapter 5: Ratios, Rates, and Proportions

“Mathematics is the art of giving the same name to different things.”

— Henri Poincaré
(And ratios are how we compare them.)

When you double a recipe, compare prices per ounce at the grocery store, or calculate how fast you’re driving—you’re using ratios and rates. This chapter teaches you to recognize, write, and reason with these powerful tools—and to solve problems where quantities change in relation to one another.

5.1 Ratios and Their Meanings

A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind.

Ways to Write a Ratio

  • Using the word “to”: 3 to 5
  • With a colon: 3 : 5
  • As a fraction: \(\frac{3}{5}\)

Important: Order matters!
“Boys to girls = 3 : 5” is not the same as “girls to boys = 5 : 3”.

Types of Ratios

  • Part-to-part: Compares one part to another (e.g., red marbles to blue marbles).
  • Part-to-whole: Compares a part to the total (e.g., red marbles to all marbles).

Example:
In a bag: 4 red, 6 blue → total = 10

  • Part-to-part (red : blue) = 4 : 6 = 2 : 3 (simplified)
  • Part-to-whole (red : total) = 4 : 10 = 2 : 5

Practical Insight:
Ratios don’t tell you the actual size—just the relationship.
A 2:3 paint mix could be 2 cups + 3 cups… or 20 gallons + 30 gallons. The proportion is what matters.

5.2 Rates and Unit Rates

A rate compares two quantities of different kinds, often involving time, cost, or distance.

Examples:

  • 60 miles per 2 hours
  • $4.50 for 3 pounds
  • 240 heartbeats in 4 minutes

A unit rate expresses the rate per one unit of the second quantity. This makes comparisons easy.

How to Find a Unit Rate

Divide the first quantity by the second.

Examples:

  • Speed: \( \frac{60\ \text{miles}}{2\ \text{hours}} = 30\ \text{miles per hour} \)
  • Price: \( \frac{\$4.50}{3\ \text{lb}} = \$1.50\ \text{per pound} \)
  • Heart rate: \( \frac{240\ \text{beats}}{4\ \text{min}} = 60\ \text{beats per minute} \)

Real-world use:
At the supermarket, unit pricing (e.g., $/oz or $/kg) lets you find the best deal—even if packages are different sizes.

Caution:
Always include units! “30” means nothing; “30 miles per hour” tells a story.

5.3 Proportional Reasoning

Two ratios form a proportion when they are equal.

\[ \frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d} \]

This means the relationship between \(a\) and \(b\) is the same as between \(c\) and \(d\).

How to Solve a Proportion

Use cross-multiplication:

If \(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}\), then \(a \cdot d = b \cdot c\)

Example:

\[ \frac{3}{4} = \frac{x}{12} \]

Cross-multiply:
\(3 \cdot 12 = 4 \cdot x\) → \(36 = 4x\) → \(x = 9\)

✅ Check: \(\frac{3}{4} = \frac{9}{12} = 0.75\) → correct.

What Makes a Relationship Proportional?

  • The graph is a straight line through the origin (0,0)
  • The unit rate is constant
  • Doubling one quantity doubles the other

Non-example:
Cell phone plan: $20 base + $0.10 per text → not proportional (doesn’t pass through origin).

5.4 Applications in Real-World Problems

Let’s apply these ideas to everyday situations.

1. Scaling Recipes

A cookie recipe uses 2 cups flour for 24 cookies. How much for 60 cookies?

Set up a proportion:

\[ \frac{2\ \text{cups}}{24\ \text{cookies}} = \frac{x}{60} \]

Cross-multiply: \(2 \cdot 60 = 24x\) → \(120 = 24x\) → \(x = 5\)

✅ Answer: 5 cups of flour

2. Map Scales

A map scale is 1 inch : 50 miles. If two cities are 3.2 inches apart on the map, how far in reality?

\[ \frac{1\ \text{in}}{50\ \text{mi}} = \frac{3.2}{x} \Rightarrow x = 50 \cdot 3.2 = 160 \]

✅ Answer: 160 miles

3. Fuel Efficiency

Your car gets 28 miles per gallon. How far can you go on 12.5 gallons?

This is a unit rate problem:
\(28\ \text{mi/gal} \times 12.5\ \text{gal} = 350\ \text{miles}\)

✅ Answer: 350 miles

4. Currency Exchange

$1 USD = 0.93 EUR. How many euros for $250?

\[ 250 \times 0.93 = 232.5 \]

✅ Answer: €232.50

Problem-Solving Strategy:
1. Identify the known ratio or rate
2. Decide what you’re solving for
3. Set up a proportion or multiply by the unit rate
4. Include units and check if the answer makes sense

Chapter Summary

  • Ratios compare quantities (part-to-part or part-to-whole).
  • Rates compare different kinds of quantities; unit rates standardize to “per one.”
  • Proportions state that two ratios are equal—solve with cross-multiplication.
  • Real-world applications: cooking, travel, shopping, finance, and more rely on proportional thinking.
