Chapter 2: Basic Operations

“The essence of mathematics is in its freedom.”

— Georg Cantor

Numbers alone are like letters without words. To make them do something—to model, predict, build, or budget—we need operations: the actions we perform on numbers. This chapter covers the four foundational operations and the rules that keep them consistent, efficient, and universally understood.

2.1 Addition and Subtraction

Addition combines quantities.
Subtraction finds the difference or removes a quantity.

Addition: Putting Things Together

\[ 5 + 3 = 8 \]

You have 5 apples, someone gives you 3 more → now you have 8.

  • Key idea: Addition is counting forward on the number line.

Subtraction: Taking Away or Comparing

\[ 9 - 4 = 5 \]

You had $9, spent $4 → $5 remain.
Or: The temperature dropped from 9°C to 4°C—a change of 5°C.

  • Key idea: Subtraction is counting backward—or measuring distance between two points.

Practical Insight:
Subtraction isn’t just “taking away.” It’s also used to answer:
– How much more? (e.g., “I have 12, you have 7—how many more do I have?” → 12 – 7 = 5)
– What’s missing? (e.g., “The recipe needs 2 cups; I’ve added 1.3—how much more?”)

Watch out for:
Negative results: \( 3 - 7 = -4 \). This is valid! It means you’re 4 units below zero.

2.2 Multiplication and Division

Multiplication: Repeated Addition (and Scaling)

\[ 4 \times 6 = 24 \]

This can mean:

  • Add 6 four times: \( 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 24 \)
  • Or: 4 groups of 6 items → 24 total

But multiplication is more powerful than repeated addition. It also scales:

  • A photo enlarged to 200% becomes 2 × its original size.
  • A salary doubled is ×2.

Division: Sharing or Grouping

\[ 20 \div 5 = 4 \]

Two interpretations:

  1. Sharing: 20 cookies shared equally among 5 people → each gets 4.
  2. Grouping: How many groups of 5 can you make from 20? → 4 groups.

Important: Division by zero is undefined.
Why? There’s no number you can multiply by 0 to get 20.
“How many zeros fit into 20?” → It makes no logical sense.

Real-world caution:
When dividing money, measurements, or time, always ask: What does the quotient represent?
Example: 100 miles ÷ 2 hours = 50 miles per hour—a rate, not just a number.

2.3 Order of Operations (PEMDAS)

When multiple operations appear in one expression, we need rules to avoid confusion.

PEMDAS is a memory aid:

  • Parentheses
  • Exponents
  • Multiplication & Division (left to right)
  • Addition & Subtraction (left to right)

⚠️ Common mistake: Thinking multiplication always comes before division.
Truth: They have equal priority—go left to right.

Example:

\[ 8 + 3 \times 2 - 6 \div 3 \]

Step-by-step:

  1. Multiply: \( 3 \times 2 = 6 \)
  2. Divide: \( 6 \div 3 = 2 \)
  3. Now: \( 8 + 6 - 2 \)
  4. Add: \( 8 + 6 = 14 \)
  5. Subtract: \( 14 - 2 = 12 \)

✅ Answer: 12

Why it matters:
In programming, finance, or engineering, a misplaced operation can crash a rocket or overcharge a customer. PEMDAS is your safety protocol.

2.4 Math Properties: The Rules That Make Math Work

These properties aren’t just abstract—they make mental math faster and algebra possible.

1. Commutative Property

  • Addition: \( a + b = b + a \) → \( 7 + 3 = 3 + 7 \)
  • Multiplication: \( a \times b = b \times a \) → \( 5 \times 4 = 4 \times 5 \)

❌ Does NOT work for subtraction or division:
\( 10 - 4 \ne 4 - 10 \), and \( 12 \div 3 \ne 3 \div 12 \)

2. Associative Property

  • Addition: \( (a + b) + c = a + (b + c) \) → \( (2 + 3) + 4 = 2 + (3 + 4) = 9 \)
  • Multiplication: \( (a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c) \)

Useful for regrouping:
\( 25 \times 4 \times 7 = (25 \times 4) \times 7 = 100 \times 7 = 700 \)

3. Distributive Property

\[ a \times (b + c) = a \times b + a \times c \]

Example:

\[ 6 \times (10 + 3) = 6 \times 10 + 6 \times 3 = 60 + 18 = 78 \]

Superpower: This is the foundation of algebra and mental math.
Calculate \( 12 \times 19 \) as \( 12 \times (20 - 1) = 240 - 12 = 228 \)

4. Identity Properties

  • Additive identity: \( a + 0 = a \) → Adding zero changes nothing.
  • Multiplicative identity: \( a \times 1 = a \) → Multiplying by one keeps the number intact.

5. Zero Property of Multiplication

\[ a \times 0 = 0 \]

No matter how big \( a \) is, multiplying by zero gives zero.

Life analogy:
If you earn $1,000 per hour but work 0 hours, you earn $0.

Chapter Summary

  • Addition/Subtraction: Combine or compare quantities.
  • Multiplication/Division: Scale, repeat, share, or group.
  • PEMDAS: The universal order that prevents ambiguity.
  • Properties: The hidden logic that makes computation flexible and algebra possible.
