Chapter 1: Extending the Number Line

“The introduction of negative numbers is one of the most profound ideas in elementary mathematics—because it expands reality, not just notation.”

— Professor’s Reflection

So far, you’ve worked with numbers that count, measure, and compare. But the real world includes losses, depths, debts, and directions—concepts that require numbers less than zero. In this chapter, we formally extend the number line into the negative, define key properties, and master operations with negative numbers—including fractions.

1.1 Review of Integers and Opposites

The integers are:

\[ \dots, -4, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, \dots \]
  • Positive integers: to the right of 0
  • Negative integers: to the left of 0
  • Zero: neither positive nor negative; the origin

Opposites

Every number has an opposite—the same distance from 0, but on the other side.

  • Opposite of \(5\) is \(-5\)
  • Opposite of \(-3\) is \(3\)
  • Opposite of \(0\) is \(0\)

We write the opposite of \(a\) as \(-a\).
So: \(-(-4) = 4\)

Real-world analogy:
If +50 ft means 50 feet above sea level, then –50 ft means 50 feet below. They are opposites in elevation—but equal in magnitude.

1.2 Negative Numbers and Absolute Value

Negative Numbers in Context

  • Finance: –$200 = $200 of debt
  • Temperature: –10°C = 10 degrees below freezing
  • Elevation: –282 ft = 282 feet below sea level (Death Valley)
  • Direction: –5 miles = 5 miles west (if east is positive)

Absolute Value: Distance from Zero

The absolute value of a number is its distance from 0 on the number line—always non-negative.

Notation: \( |x| \)

Examples:

  • \( |7| = 7 \)
  • \( |-7| = 7 \)
  • \( |0| = 0 \)

Key idea: Absolute value strips away sign, keeping only size.

Practical use:

  • “The temperature changed by 12 degrees” → \( |\text{final} - \text{initial}| = 12 \), regardless of warming or cooling.
  • Error tolerance: “Acceptable length is 10 cm ± 0.5 cm” → \( |x - 10| \leq 0.5 \)

1.3 Adding, Subtracting, Multiplying, and Dividing Negatives

Let’s master the rules—with reasoning, not just memorization.

Addition

  • Same signs: Add magnitudes, keep sign
    \[ (-4) + (-6) = -10 \quad ; \quad 3 + 7 = 10 \]
  • Different signs: Subtract magnitudes, keep sign of the larger absolute value
    \[ (-8) + 5 = -3 \quad ; \quad (-2) + 9 = 7 \]

Number line view:
Start at the first number, then move right for positive, left for negative.

Subtraction

Subtracting a number = Adding its opposite

\[ a - b = a + (-b) \]

Examples:

  • \( 5 - 8 = 5 + (-8) = -3 \)
  • \( -4 - (-7) = -4 + 7 = 3 \)
  • \( -6 - 2 = -6 + (-2) = -8 \)

Mnemonic: “Keep, Change, Change”
Keep the first number, change subtraction to addition, change the sign of the second number.

Multiplication & Division

  • Same signs → positive result
    \[ (-3) \times (-4) = 12 \quad ; \quad (-15) \div (-3) = 5 \]
  • Different signs → negative result
    \[ 6 \times (-2) = -12 \quad ; \quad (-20) \div 4 = -5 \]

Why does negative × negative = positive?
Think of it as reversing a reversal.
Example: “I don’t not like it” = I do like it.
Or mathematically:
\[ (-1) \times (-1) = 1 \quad \text{to preserve the distributive property} \]

Caution:
\(-3^2\) means \(-(3^2) = -9\), but \((-3)^2 = 9\). Parentheses matter!

1.4 Negative Fractions and Mixed Numbers

Fractions can be negative too! The negative sign can appear in three places—all equivalent:

\[ -\frac{3}{4} = \frac{-3}{4} = \frac{3}{-4} \]

But never in the denominator alone in final answers—convention places the sign in front or on the numerator.

Operations with Negative Fractions

All rules from Section 1.3 apply.

Example 1 (Addition):

\[ -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{3}{4} = \frac{-2}{4} + \frac{3}{4} = \frac{1}{4} \]

Example 2 (Multiplication):

\[ -\frac{2}{5} \times \frac{7}{3} = -\frac{14}{15} \]

Example 3 (Division):

\[ -\frac{3}{8} \div \left(-\frac{1}{4}\right) = -\frac{3}{8} \times \left(-\frac{4}{1}\right) = \frac{12}{8} = \frac{3}{2} = 1\frac{1}{2} \]

Negative Mixed Numbers

Write as improper fractions before operating.

\[ -2\frac{1}{3} = -\left(2 + \frac{1}{3}\right) = -\frac{7}{3} \]

Important:
\(-2\frac{1}{3}\) means negative of \(2\frac{1}{3}\)—not \(-2 + \frac{1}{3}\).
Always treat the whole mixed number as a single quantity.

Chapter Summary

  • The number line extends infinitely in both directions—negative numbers model real-world opposites.
  • Absolute value measures distance from zero, ignoring direction.
  • Operations with negatives:
    • Addition/Subtraction: Use opposites and number line logic
    • Multiplication/Division: Same signs → positive; different → negative
  • Negative fractions follow the same rules—just track the sign carefully.
