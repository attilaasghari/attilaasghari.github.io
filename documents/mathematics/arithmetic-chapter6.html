6.1 Interest: Simple and Compound

When you save money or borrow it, interest is the cost (or reward) of using that money over time.

Simple Interest

Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount (the principal).

Formula:

\[ I = P \cdot r \cdot t \]
  • \(I\) = interest earned or paid
  • \(P\) = principal (initial amount)
  • \(r\) = annual interest rate (as a decimal)
  • \(t\) = time in years

Example:
You deposit \$1,200 in a savings account at 3% simple interest for 4 years.

\[ I = 1200 \cdot 0.03 \cdot 4 = 144 \]

Total amount = \(1200 + 144 = \$1,344\)

✅ Simple interest is common in short-term loans or basic savings accounts.

Compound Interest (Introduction)

Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus any previously earned interest—so your money “grows faster.”

Basic idea:

  • Year 1: Interest on \$1,000
  • Year 2: Interest on \$1,000 + Year 1 interest
  • And so on…

Formula (annual compounding):

\[ A = P(1 + r)^t \]
  • \(A\) = total amount after \(t\) years

Example:
\$1,000 at 5% compounded annually for 3 years:

\[ A = 1000(1 + 0.05)^3 = 1000(1.157625) = \$1,157.63 \]

Why it matters:
Over time, compound interest can dramatically outpace simple interest—this is the power behind long-term investing (and the danger of high-interest debt).

Rule of Thumb:
“Compound interest is the eighth wonder of the world. He who understands it, earns it… he who doesn’t, pays it.” — often attributed to Einstein

6.2 Basic Statistics: Mean, Median, Mode, Range

When you see “average salary” or “typical temperature,” you’re encountering descriptive statistics—ways to summarize data.

Given a data set:
\[ 5,\ 7,\ 7,\ 8,\ 10,\ 12,\ 15 \]

Mean (Average)

Add all values, divide by how many:

\[ \text{Mean} = \frac{5 + 7 + 7 + 8 + 10 + 12 + 15}{7} = \frac{64}{7} \approx 9.14 \]

Median (Middle Value)

Order the data; pick the center number.

  • Odd count: middle value → 8
  • Even count: average of two middle values

Mode (Most Frequent)

The value that appears most often → 7

Range (Spread)

Difference between max and min:

\[ 15 - 5 = 10 \]

Practical Insight:
– Mean is sensitive to outliers (e.g., one billionaire skews “average income”).
– Median better represents “typical” in skewed data (e.g., house prices).
– Mode is useful for categories (e.g., most common shoe size).

6.3 Inequalities: Understanding >, <, ≥, ≤

Not all comparisons are equal—and that’s where inequalities come in.

Symbol Meaning Example
< Less than \(3 < 5\)
> Greater than \(10 > 2\)
≤ Less than or equal “You must be ≤ 12 to order the kids’ meal”
≥ Greater than or equal “Score ≥ 70 to pass”

Key Ideas

  • On a number line, < and ≤ point left; > and ≥ point right.
  • When you multiply or divide both sides by a negative number, flip the inequality sign.

Example:

\[ -2x > 6 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x < -3 \quad \text{(sign flipped!)} \]

Real-world use:
Budgeting: “I can spend ≤ \$50 on groceries.”
Age restrictions: “Ride requires height ≥ 48 inches.”

6.4 Word Problems and Practical Reasoning

Word problems aren’t puzzles—they’re translations of real situations into math.

A 4-Step Strategy

  1. Understand: What is being asked? What information is given?
  2. Plan: What operation, formula, or relationship applies?
  3. Solve: Do the math carefully. Track units.
  4. Check: Does the answer make sense? Is it reasonable?

Example Problem

Maria earns \$12.50/hour. She works 35 hours this week and pays 15% in taxes. How much does she take home?

  • Step 1: Find gross pay → \(12.50 \times 35 = 437.50\)
  • Step 2: Find tax → \(0.15 \times 437.50 = 65.625\)
  • Step 3: Net pay → \(437.50 - 65.63 = 371.87\)
  • Step 4: Check: ~\$372 for 35 hours → ~\$10.60/hour after tax → reasonable.

Pro Tip:
Underline key numbers and phrases: “15% of,” “total cost,” “per mile,” “at least.”

Common Word Problem Types

  • Money: discounts, tax, tips, wages
  • Time & Rate: distance = rate × time
  • Mixtures: combining solutions or ingredients
  • Comparison: “twice as many,” “5 more than”

Mindset:
Every word problem is a story. Your job is to find the math hidden inside it.

  5. You invest \$2,000 at 6% compounded annually. How much after 2 years?
  6. A student scored 85, 90, 78, and 92 on four tests. What score is needed on the fifth to have an average of 88?
